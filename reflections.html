My learning journey

Reflections on Ecosystem Studies

Each reflection explores a different aspect of my learning. The focus is not only on what I studied, but also on what I understood, questioned, observed, and could apply elsewhere.

01

Understanding Ecosystems as Connected Systems

One of the key lessons I am taking away from Ecosystem Studies is that ecosystems cannot be understood by looking at their components in isolation. Organisms, soil, water, climate, and human activities influence one another.

This perspective has helped me think about environmental changes as interconnected processes rather than isolated events. It also makes me consider how a change in one component can affect the functioning of the wider system.

CONCEPTUAL UNDERSTANDING
02

Learning from Field Observations

Fieldwork provides an opportunity to connect theoretical concepts with actual environmental conditions. Observing vegetation, habitat characteristics, and human influences can reveal details that may be difficult to appreciate through classroom learning alone.

My field observations have encouraged me to pay closer attention to the differences between locations and to consider how ecological conditions influence the organisms found there.

FIELDWORK AND OBSERVATION
03

Connecting Field Data with Ecological Patterns

Collecting ecological data is only the first step. Organising, comparing, and interpreting observations can help identify patterns in species distribution, abundance, and environmental conditions.

This has helped me appreciate the importance of recording data systematically, recognising limitations in sampling, and distinguishing between an observed pattern and a conclusion that the data can actually support.

ANALYTICAL LEARNING
04

Bringing Ecosystem Studies into GIS

My interest in GIS and remote sensing gives me another way to approach ecological questions. Satellite imagery and spatial analysis can help examine vegetation, land-use change, and the distribution of environmental features across larger areas.

Ecosystem Studies provides the ecological context needed to interpret these spatial patterns. A mapped change becomes more meaningful when I can consider the ecological processes that might explain it.

CROSS-DISCIPLINARY LEARNING
05

People, Landscapes, and Sustainability

Ecosystems are influenced by human decisions as well as natural processes. Urban expansion, land-use change, resource use, and habitat modification can affect ecological relationships and ecosystem functioning.

Thinking about these interactions has made me more aware of the need to consider environmental consequences when evaluating development and conservation. It also connects ecosystem learning with the broader goals of sustainability.

HUMAN–ENVIRONMENT INTERACTIONS
06

Learning Through Collaboration

Discussions and collaborative activities can introduce different ways of interpreting the same environmental problem. Working with others can help identify gaps in my understanding and encourage me to explain my reasoning more clearly.

These experiences also highlight the value of combining different observations and perspectives when studying complex ecological systems.

GROUP WORK AND COMMUNICATION

A closer look

Linking ecological knowledge with real-world questions

An important part of my learning is recognising that ecological concepts become more useful when applied to specific environmental questions.

For example, studying vegetation around a lake involves more than identifying plants. It can also involve considering habitat conditions, species interactions, human disturbance, and changes in the surrounding landscape.

Combining field observations with spatial data can help build a more complete understanding of these relationships. At the same time, both field sampling and satellite-derived measurements have limitations that need to be considered when drawing conclusions.

Looking back

What I am taking forward

These are the broader lessons I want to carry into future coursework, research, and environmental projects.

01 · Think in systems

Look for relationships between organisms, environmental conditions, and human activities rather than considering each factor independently.

02 · Question the data

Consider sampling methods, scale, uncertainty, and limitations before interpreting patterns or making claims.

03 · Connect disciplines

Use ecological understanding alongside field observations, GIS, remote sensing, and quantitative analysis.

04 · Observe critically

Pay attention to environmental differences and ask what processes could explain the conditions observed in the field.

05 · Communicate clearly

Present observations, methods, results, and interpretations in a way that makes their meaning and limitations clear.

06 · Consider sustainability

Evaluate environmental questions with attention to ecosystem functioning, conservation, and human well-being.
